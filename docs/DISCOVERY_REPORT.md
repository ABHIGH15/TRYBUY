# TRYBUY — Discovery & Research Report

## Executive conclusion
**Recommendation: DO NOT BUILD TRYBUY AS A FAKE-SHOPPING SIMULATOR.**

The phenomenon is real, interesting and currently receiving attention, particularly around South Korea's 2026 “dopamine sites.” But the category is already populated by multiple simulators, including Fake Shopping App, MyShopping, FakeShop, DopamineKart, Dopamine Shop and FakeMall.

More importantly, a fake-shopping simulator has a weak connection to the stated marketplace hypothesis:
`browsing → expressed intent → better discovery/personalization → future purchase`

The simulator primarily creates entertainment/recreation. It does not inherently create a defensible marketplace advantage.

The research does, however, support a much more interesting underlying observation:
*Shopping is not always a transactional activity. People browse, explore, compare, imagine ownership and save products even when they are not ready to purchase.*

Research has long distinguished utilitarian and hedonic shopping motivations, with hedonic motivations influencing exploratory information seeking and browsing behaviour. More recent work finds that online shoppers can switch between goal-directed shopping and serendipitous browsing, meaning that the same user can have very different shopping intentions within the same platform.

That suggests a potentially valuable problem:
**E-commerce platforms are very good at knowing what users clicked. They may be less good at knowing why the user is interested in something and what they want to happen next.**

That is where TryBuy may have a real opportunity.

But there is another major caveat:
**A simple “better wishlist” is also not enough.**
Myntra already has Wishlist and a dedicated Price Drop experience. Amazon has Lists/Wish Lists, Flipkart has Wishlist and Save for Later, and other platforms already provide similar mechanisms.

Therefore the strongest current direction is:
**TRYBUY = an intent-aware shopping layer that helps users express what they are exploring, why they care, and when they may want to act — while giving a marketplace a richer signal than passive browsing alone.**

This is currently a hypothesis, not a validated product.

---

### 1. The original problem
The original concept began with:
“People sometimes enjoy shopping even when they don't intend to buy.”
This is supported.

Research on online shopping has identified both utilitarian and hedonic motivations. Hedonic shopping motivation is associated with exploratory information seeking, impulse buying and longer pre-purchase browsing.
A 2026 study on exploratory behaviour similarly found that users can spend longer than anticipated browsing online stores and that browsing goals and modes influence navigation and retention.

So:
**FACT:** Browsing can itself provide value.

But the original product interpretation:
“Therefore, let's build a fake shopping app.”
does not automatically follow.

That is an important product distinction.
*   **Problem A**: People enjoy shopping without buying. (Evidence: reasonably strong)
*   **Problem B**: Existing commerce experiences do not adequately support non-purchase exploration. (Evidence: plausible, but not yet sufficiently established)
*   **Problem C**: People want a dedicated fake shopping environment. (Evidence: emerging but still uncertain)
*   **Problem D**: A marketplace can turn non-purchase browsing into future commercial value through better intent signals. (Evidence: theoretically strong, but TryBuy-specific value remains unproven)

These four statements must not be conflated.

### 2. The “fake shopping” category is real
The original assumption that fake shopping is merely an invented concept is no longer correct.
In 2026, a visible category of “dopamine sites” emerged in South Korea. These services simulate shopping or food delivery without actual transactions. Users browse, build carts, simulate checkout and sometimes watch fictional delivery tracking.
The phenomenon has received coverage from international media, including the Washington Post and Financial Times.
Community discussions also show that people already engage in similar behaviour organically: filling carts, browsing products for long periods and abandoning purchases.

There is therefore genuine behavioural evidence behind the concept.
But there is a problem. **The category is already being productized.**
Current examples include:
*   Fake Shopping App
*   MyShopping
*   FakeShop
*   DopamineKart
*   Dopamine Shop
*   FakeMall
*   ZeroRupees
*   various Korean “dopamine” sites

**Product verdict:**
*   Fake shopping as a concept: VALID.
*   Fake shopping as TryBuy's differentiation: WEAK.
*   We should not spend months building a more polished version of something that is already becoming a recognizable category.

### 3. What psychology actually tells us
We should also be careful with the phrase “dopamine shopping.”
The popular narrative often reduces the phenomenon to: `shopping → dopamine → happiness`
That is too simplistic.

Research on online compulsive buying does demonstrate the importance of shopping-related cues, craving and affective responses, but that is different from saying that ordinary browsing is simply a “dopamine hit.”

The safer product interpretation is:
**Shopping can provide hedonic value, exploration, anticipation, imagination and stimulation independent of immediate ownership.**
That is much more defensible.

There is also an important ethical issue. A product deliberately optimized to maximize the psychological reward of shopping could potentially reproduce the very compulsive behaviour it claims to replace.

Therefore I would not position TryBuy as: “Get your shopping dopamine without spending.”
Instead: **“Explore what you like without the pressure to buy.”**
That is considerably stronger from a consumer-product perspective.

### 4. The bigger opportunity: intent
This is where the research becomes much more interesting.
E-commerce systems already observe enormous amounts of implicit behaviour (impressions, clicks, searches, wishlist additions, etc.).
Research shows that browsing/clickstream data can be used to predict purchase intent. Amazon researchers have even described systems that infer higher-level shopping intents from online behaviour and use those inferred intents to personalize recommendations.

So the business premise: “Intent can be valuable to an e-commerce marketplace” is not speculative.
The speculative part is:
**“TryBuy can generate a meaningfully better intent signal than what the marketplace already has.”**

### 5. Why a normal wishlist isn't enough
At first glance, TryBuy sounds identical to Wishlist. And that is a serious problem.
Myntra, Amazon, and Flipkart already have wishlists, lists, save for later, and price drop sections.

Therefore: “Save products you like and buy them later” is not a product gap.

There is also interesting academic evidence that the wishlist itself is not necessarily a clean purchase-intent signal. Research found that putting a product onto a wishlist can actually reduce purchase intent in some circumstances because users defer the decision.

So: **Wishlist ≠ purchase intent.**

### 6. The potential gap
The more interesting gap is not: “Where can I save this?”
It is: **“What does my saving this mean?”**

Imagine a user sees a ₹2,999 jacket. They may:
A. Want it now but can't afford it.
B. Like it but want to compare alternatives.
C. Need it for a wedding next month.
D. Like the style but not this exact product.
E. Want something similar at ₹1,500.
F. Are simply browsing for inspiration.
G. Want to buy only if it goes below ₹2,000.
H. Are considering buying it but need to check sizing.

Today, a “heart” or wishlist addition collapses all of those states into roughly the same signal: “User saved product.”
That is potentially the product gap.

### 7. The key TryBuy hypothesis
If users can express lightweight shopping intent beyond simply saving a product, the marketplace can provide more relevant future discovery and re-engagement while the user gets a more useful way to manage non-immediate shopping decisions.

**User:** “I don't have to decide now.” but also: “I won't lose the context of why I liked it.”
**Marketplace:** “I learn more than just what the user clicked.”

### 8. But there is an important objection
“Why can't Myntra just add these fields to Wishlist?”
If the answer is: “They could.” then TryBuy is not a standalone product.

TryBuy may ultimately be better understood as **a new shopping interaction / intent layer** rather than a new standalone e-commerce marketplace. For a product case study, it could be stronger.

### 9. Competitive landscape
| Product/category | Core job | What it already solves | TryBuy implication |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Myntra Wishlist | Save products | Save future purchases | Cannot compete on saving |
| Myntra Price Drop | Discover discounted items | Price-triggered re-engagement | Cannot own basic price alerts |
| Amazon Lists | Organize future purchases | Lists, sharing, saving | Basic organization already solved |
| Flipkart Save for Later | Delay purchase | Remove item from active cart | Basic postponement already solved |
| Fake shopping apps | Recreational shopping | Simulated shopping ritual | Fake checkout is not differentiated |
| Pinterest/social discovery | Inspiration | Discovery/aspiration | Discovery itself is crowded |
| Donut | Cross-store shopping | Comparison + universal wishlist + alerts | Cross-marketplace saving is crowded |
| **TryBuy proposed** | **Explicit intent** | **Captures why/when/how a user likes** | **Potential whitespace** |

### 10. The Indian market makes the problem interesting
Google/Deloitte's 2026 India commerce report projects the e-commerce market to reach $250B by 2030, with Gen Z accounting for 45% of online spend. The traditional linear shopping funnel is becoming an always-on cycle of discovery, validation and purchase.
The emerging environment looks more like: Content → Discovery → Exploration → Comparison → Validation → Intent → Purchase.

### 11-15. Five possible TryBuy directions
1.  **Fake Shopping Simulator**: Kill. High feasibility, low differentiation/value.
2.  **Smart Wishlist / Intent Wishlist**: Strong. Instead of ❤️ Save, users express lightweight intent.
3.  **Shopping Decision Companion**: Strongest. Helps user with "I'm interested, but I'm not ready". Creates a decision memory.
4.  **Exploration Mode**: Interesting. A distinct mode optimized for serendipity and inspiration over fast checkout.
5.  **Personal Shopping Memory**: Long-term. Persistent layer of shopping context for highly contextual recommendations.

### 16. What I would currently build (Working Hypothesis)
**TRYBUY helps shoppers express and manage “I’m interested, but I’m not ready to buy” intent.**
The product should answer three questions:
1.  What are you interested in?
2.  Why are you interested?
3.  What would make you act?

**DISCOVER → EXPRESS INTENT → REMEMBER CONTEXT → WAIT / EXPLORE / COMPARE → RE-ENGAGE WHEN RELEVANT → DECIDE → BUY OR DISMISS**

TryBuy should not manufacture a purchase. It should help the user make a better decision.

### 17-19. The critical experiment
Control: Normal shopping experience + Wishlist.
Treatment: Normal shopping experience + TryBuy intent interaction.

*Example:* After saving, ask "What are you waiting for?" (Just browsing, Waiting for a better price, Comparing alternatives, Need it later, Looking for something similar).
*Measure:* Subsequent meaningful shopping action, intent completion rate, return visits.
This tests: Does explicitly expressed intent create better downstream outcomes than passive wishlist behaviour?

### 20. Biggest risks
*   Users don't want to express intent (too much friction).
*   Existing behavioural data is already sufficient.
*   It becomes a glorified wishlist.
*   Business value is too indirect.

### 21. Important branding problem
**TryBuy.in** is already an active Indian fashion brand. TRYBUY must be treated as a working codename, not the final brand.

### 22-24. Current product thesis & Verdict
“A significant part of digital shopping happens before purchase... TryBuy explores whether lightweight explicit intent can help shoppers manage non-immediate decisions while giving marketplaces a more useful signal for future discovery and personalization.”

**Verdict:**
Original Fake shopping simulator → **KILL**
Revised Intent-aware exploration layer → **CONTINUE RESEARCH**

### 25. The next research phase
1.  **User research**: Discover if people actually experience the problem.
2.  **Competitive teardown**: Deeply inspect Myntra, Amazon, Flipkart, AJIO, Meesho, Pinterest, Donut. Map the intent loss.
3.  **User interviews / survey**: Ask about actual recent behaviour, not hypothetical product use.
4.  **Prototype experiment**: Test if lightweight TryBuy intent changes behaviour.

**Bottom line:** Do not code the current idea. Focus on explicit shopping intent, decision memory, and future discovery. The next step is actual user research, a competitive teardown, followed by 3–5 product concepts. Treat TRYBUY as a codename.
